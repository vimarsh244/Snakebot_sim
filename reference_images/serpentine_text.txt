Reinforcement Learning of Serpentine Locomotion for a Snake Robot
Ke Qiu, Hang Zhang, Yikai Lv, Yunkai Wang, Chunlin Zhou†, Rong Xiong†
Abstract— The locomotion control of the snake robot is a
challenging task that involves many degrees of freedom. Con-
sider the rhythmic behaviour, we introduce the central pattern
generator (CPG) model to provide serpentine locomotion, since
it can reduce the control dimensionality while remaining contin-
uous and ﬂexible. However, the model parameters are usually
chosen empirically, which is time consuming and complicated.
Thus, we apply deep deterministic policy gradient (DDPG)
algorithm of reinforcement learning (RL) to tune the model and
obtain optimal parameters. Consequently, the hyper-redundant
snake robot performs graceful serpentine locomotion with an
RL controller in simulation. The ﬂexibility of our CPG model
and the feasibility of DDPG algorithm are veriﬁed according
to the experimental results.
I. INTRODUCTION
The snake is born an experienced predator and an expert
athlete. It is able to travel on forest ﬂoor, on desert and
in water, exhibiting excellent mobility in various types of
terrains. Inspired by the snake, snake robots are invented.
They usually have many degrees of freedom, and move
without the help of wheels or legs [11]. In 1972, Shigeo
Hirose constructed the ﬁrst snake robot called Active Cord
Mechanism and gave a good overview [2]. In 2009, Stavdahl
Øyvind et al. provided a snake robot with a contact force
sensing system that is able to realize obstacle-aided locomo-
tion [3]. In 2012, Howie Choset et al. proposed the Uniﬁed
Snake that is capable of traversing diverse environments
[4]. The small cross section and serpentine body allows
snake robots to take part in rescue tasks or inspection
operations, especially in narrow and conﬁned spaces, and
hyper-redundant mechanism provides plenty of performance.
Many approaches to make the locomotion of snake robot
are being developed in the meantime. In 1972, Hirose
modelled a snake using wheeled links with no side slip and
proposed a serpenoid curve to describe the gliding motion
of a snake, which is the most common form of snake
locomotion [3]. In 2012, Howie Choset et al. used a snake
robot with sixteen degrees of freedom to present a variety of
gaits over ﬂat ground by using the virtual chassis method,
that is, to treat the snake robot like a wheeled robot [5].
Motoyasu Tanaka focused mainly on the three-dimensional
steering and stair climbing of snake robots with switching

In 2020, a model-free RL algorithm was introduced for

As serpentine locomotion exhibits typical rhythmic pat-
terns, the artiﬁcial central pattern generator (CPG) is nat-
urally be adopted as a fundamental kinematic framework
for the motion control of snake robots. It can perform
periodic behaviours and maintain smoothness while param-

networks, thus being widely used for robot locomotion like
the hexapod [10] or the ﬁsh [11]. In 2006, Alessandro Crespi
et al. constructed an amphibious snake robot AmphiBot II
controlled by an on-board CPG inspired by those found
in vertebrates [12]. Zhelong Wang et al. applied a CPG
model on a snake robot, adjusting the speed and direction by
modulating the parameters [13] in 2016. Particularly, Ijspeert
et al. used a robotic salamander and a spinal cord model to
explain the ability of salamanders to switch between swim-
ming and walking [14]. Then in 2019, deep deterministic
policy gradient (DDPG) algorithm was used to this robotic
salamander to smoothly move toward a desired target by
adapting to the environmental change from the ﬁrm ground
to the mud with the same spinal cord model [15].
This paper proposed to combine a CPG model and our
snake robot as a basic kinematic framework for serpentine
locomotion because of the efﬁciency of generating rhythmic
behaviour and the similarity to the salamander robot. Then,
we apply DDPG algorithm to learn to determine proper
parameters for a certain goal, instead of directly mapping
to joint angular positions. By this way, the motion can be
more natural and the control simpler. Experimental results
of the simulation validate our scenario.
The paper is organized as follows. In section II we
combine a mathematical CPG model with our modular and
hyper-redundant snake robot. In section III, the platform,
observation space, action space and reward function are given
to explain the learning procedure for rhythmic locomotion.
The results of learning and the comparison of whether using
an RL controller are presented in section IV. Conclusions
are drawn in section V.

A. Central Pattern Generator
CPG is a neural network capable of producing coordinated
patterns of rhythmic activities, without any inputs from
sensory feedback or higher-level centres [16]. It has been
widely discovered in many creatures and form a fundamen-
tal architecture of rhythmic activities like swimming and
walking [17], [18]. CPGs that are distributed in different
segments can be also organized as a coupled network. For
most creatures, rich types of locomotion are needed, and thus
higher-level centres take responsibility for modulating CPGs
in order to generate desired patterns. Under the guidance
of neurobiology, CPG has been translated into mathematical
model and has been increasingly used in the control of
robot locomotion. Different types of neurobiological models
include neuron models like [19] and oscillator models. The
latter is on the basis of mathematical models of nonlinear
differential equations such as phase oscillator [20], [21] and
Matsuoka oscillator [22]. A spinal cord model has been
constructed and applied to a salamander robot in order to
switch gait between swimming and walking. See [14] for
the implementation.
B. Structure of The Snake Robot
The snake robot contains eight independent modules and
each module has two degrees of freedom. As seen in Fig.1,
the components to constitute a module are two servo motors,
several motor brackets, a shell and a connector. Four motor
brackets ﬁx two servo motors on the shell with screws.
The shell in circular shape consists of two halves that
wrap around servo motors. An aluminium connector provides
mechanical connection between modules. If we set up a co-
ordinate system and specify that positive x direction is along
the spine of the snake robot and positive z direction points
normally upwards, then the former servo motor controls the
roll angle while the latter one controls the yaw angle. Servo
motors are connected by a serial bus.
C. Central Pattern Generator Model of Our Snake Robot
On our snake robot, every joint is regarded as an oscillator
and the output signal controls the joint angle. Inspired by
the spinal cord model [14], we present ours with some
modiﬁcations. Compared with the original model, we list the
modiﬁcations below. (i) The spinal cord model uses a sine
function as an interactive term in the differential equation set.
We ﬁnd in practice that an inverse tangent function works
perfectly well due to its non-decreasing property. (ii) The
spinal cord model uses a second order differential equation
to make the amplitude approach to the target amplitude
exponentially. Our model realizes the same thing but simpler.
(iii) We use Fourier series as an output, thus able to generate
any periodic signal theoretically.

this oscillator to produce is given. Without loss of generality,
we assume that the periodic signal is an odd function, thus

III. A LEARNING POLICY FOR THE KINEMATIC
FRAMEWORK
RL is a learning paradigm concerned with learning to
control a system so as to maximize a numerical performance
measure that expresses a long-term objective [23]. The basic
idea is shown in Fig.3(a). The controller observes the state
of the system and associated reward. It then calculates and
sends an optimal action back to the system, making the
system transfer to a new state and thus repeat the cycle.
The rule maps a state to an action is called policy. Classical
value iteration method works on problems with discrete and
ﬁnite actions. The development of DDPG makes it extended
to continuous and high-dimensional action spaces. DDPG is
an algorithm that implement an actor-critic architecture via
deep neural networks. As shown in Fig.3(b), the actor aims
at improving the current policy while the critic evaluates the
current policy by a Q-function. The algorithm then performs
gradient ascent to reach the optimal parameters. We give a
brief description of the algorithm that will be applied later.
For more detailed explanation, please refer to [24].
In our task, the system is a robot platform that contains
our snake robot and the plane on which the robot moves.
The platform is constructed using PyBullet. We translate
the design above into a URDF and create corresponding
Gym environment for the snake robot. Observation space,
action space and reward function play an important role
in this environment. Denote the observation space by X,
action space by A, reward at time t by Rt. Actor and
critic are function approximations using neural networks with
parameters η and μ, that is:

IV. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
The learning procedures are carried on an Intel Core i5-
8250U processor. Each time it is trained for 10,000 steps
and at most 20 steps per episode. Five trainings take place
independently by different random seeds. In the beginning
3,000 steps, the behaviours, or state transitions precisely,
are collected in the replay buffer. After that, at the end of
every episode, the target networks are updated using the data
stored in the replay buffer. Hence, the robot initially presents

To demonstrate, we make a comparison between two
situations. In one situation, we assign empirical values to
CPG parameters, which is obtained by trials or experiences.
In another situation, we use the ﬁve RL based controllers
that we have trained with different random seeds to determine
these parameters. Every situation contains eight experiments.
We evaluate the locomotion presented by the snake robot
using the displacement along x direction. The result can be
seen in Fig.6, with every colour standing for one experiment.
In the former situation the performance of our snake robot
is always excellent because the curves in bold are at the
top. The observed gaits slightly vary from different RL
controllers, but the evaluated mobilities are close. In the
latter situation, it is of great uncertainty. Some parameters
do help the robot move forward, some make it stay put, and
some drive the snake backward. Although the dimension of
the action space is not very large currently, the performance
of the robot shows highly nonlinear property. To assign
empirical values becomes much more complicated and trial
and error becomes helpless especially when the dimension
goes higher. However, RL based controller gives stable and
efﬁcient decisions, since all the eight curves in bold increase
with an approximately constant velocity.
We still take sequential snapshots, as displayed in Fig.4.
It can be seen that the snake robot shows a smooth and

direction. Fig.7 also records the angular position of some
joints. Here, every colour represents one joint. As seen
clearly, a stable phase difference is established. As a result,
the ﬂexibility of our CPG model and the feasibility of DDPG
algorithm has been veriﬁed.
V. CONCLUSIONS
In this paper, we apply a CPG model to simplify the com-
plex control problem. Then we ﬁnd it difﬁcult to determine
the corresponding parameters of this model, considering such
an amount of redundancy. To obtain suitable parameters, RL
method is adopted to learn an optimal strategy that gives
CPG parameters, under which our snake robot can perform
graceful serpentine locomotion.
However, we are still looking for improvements. For
instance, higher order Fourier coefﬁcients can be considered
to produce more sophisticate performance, as well as other
types of network coupling. Furthermore, sensory feedback
can be integrated into differential equations of our CPG
model to help adjust output, which may realize self-tuning
in varying terrain.
